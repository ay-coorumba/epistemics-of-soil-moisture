How a soil-moisture “measurement” is actually captured.

A satellite never measures water in the ground. It catches a faint microwave glow, then a long chain of corrections, calibrations, removals and models turns that glow into the number in your file. Scroll to fly alongside one signal — from wet soil near Ayutthaya up to orbit and back down into a grid cell — with the actual working at every step: the governing equation, the numbers for SMAP, and exactly where error creeps in.

So what did we actually measure?

Every step above was necessary and, mostly, done well. But stack them up and the honest picture is that the delivered value is a skilled inference, not a reading off a dipstick. The instrument's genuine contribution — a calibrated microwave temperature — is real; almost everything that makes it useful as soil moisture is model, assumption, removal, and interpolation layered on top.

~1
genuine physical quantity measured (microwave brightness temperature)
~8
modelling, calibration & inference steps between it and “soil moisture”
0.041
the error budget in quadrature (m³/m³) — landing a hair over the 0.040 requirement

None of this means the data is bad — SMAP is superb. It means the number carries a pedigree, and using it well means knowing that pedigree: what was averaged, what was inferred, what happened inside a box nobody opens — and how each step's σ quietly joined the quadrature sum.

Worked for SMAP (L-band radiometer). The equations are the standard published forms (Fresnel emissivity, τ–ω radiative transfer, the radiometer equation, two-point calibration, kurtosis RFI detection, Backus–Gilbert gridding, τ–ω inversion, Mironov/Topp dielectric mixing, the Kalman update). The worked numbers and per-step σ values are illustrative — chosen to convey realistic magnitudes and land on the published ~0.04 m³/m³ budget — not the operational algorithm's internals. Active sensors (ASCAT, Sentinel-1) capture data differently again — that journey is told in The Echo’s Journey.
