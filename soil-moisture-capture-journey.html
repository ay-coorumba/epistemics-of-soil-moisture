How a soil-moisture “measurement” is actually captured.

A satellite never measures water in the ground. It catches a faint microwave glow, then a long chain of corrections, calibrations, removals and models turns that glow into the number in your file. So what did we actually measure?

Every step above was necessary and, mostly, done well. But stack them up and the honest picture is that the delivered value is a skilled inference, not a reading off a dipstick. The instrument's genuine contribution — a calibrated microwave temperature — is real; almost everything that makes it useful as soil moisture is model, assumption, removal, and interpolation layered on top.

~1
genuine physical quantity measured (microwave brightness temperature)
~8
modelling, calibration & inference steps between it and “soil moisture”
~10+
other datasets & models silently embedded (soil texture, land cover, weather-model temperature, dielectric constants, RFI thresholds…)

None of this means the data is bad — SMAP is superb. It means the number carries a pedigree, and using it well means knowing that pedigree: what was averaged, what was inferred, and what happened inside a box nobody opens.

Worked for SMAP (L-band radiometer). Active sensors (ASCAT, Sentinel-1) capture data differently again — they transmit a pulse and read the echo, and SAR fakes a giant antenna from the satellite's own motion — a separate journey worth its own telling. Physics is standard textbook radiometry; exact figures (footprint, NEDT, revisit) are indicative and worth a subject-matter check before publication.
